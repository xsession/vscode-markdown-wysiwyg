Why a landscape works

Your brain carries a navigation system far older than reading. Place cells in the hippocampus fire for particular spots, grid cells track how far and which way you have moved, and nearby regions pin it all to fixed landmarks. People who memorise by walking items through an imagined building, the method of loci, recall far more than by repetition. This map borrows each of those tricks.

Places that stay put. The layout comes only from the folder tree and file sizes, with no randomness, so the same project always draws the same coastline. "Auth is on the east island" stays true tomorrow.

Landmarks first. The files most others import rise as towers and stay visible even through fog, the way a spire orients you in a strange town.

Landmark, route, then survey. Wayfinding research describes learning a place as recognising landmarks, then knowing routes between them, then holding the whole layout. Towers give the first; the Route planner gives turn-by-turn directions along import roads; zooming out gives the survey view of regions and highways.

Knowing where you have been. A dotted trail and a ring on your current file do the job of path integration, so you can always retrace your steps.

Exploring builds the map. With fog of war on, land appears only where you have walked, so the map you hold in your head grows with the one on screen.

A walk you can replay. Walk mode visits the landmarks in a fixed spatial order, from the starting point outward, with compass directions between stops: a memory palace built from your own architecture.

Shape you can feel. Height is code, water is a module boundary, roads are dependencies. Heavy, tangled districts read as mountains crowded with roads before you open a single file.
